Polar-alignment fundamentals

An equatorial mount only tracks the sky if its RA axis points at the celestial pole. Here's what the error costs, how Ephemeris estimates it, and which PHD2 tool fixes it.

What polar-alignment error causes

If the mount's axis is offset from the true pole, the mount rotates about the wrong point. Two things follow:

  • Declination drift — targets slide steadily in Dec at a rate proportional to the alignment error. Guiding corrects this, but it costs a constant stream of one-directional Dec pulses (and fights backlash on the way).
  • Field rotation — the whole field slowly rotates around the guide star. Guiding cannot correct this: PHD2 keeps one point fixed while the corners arc around it. The further the target from the guide star and the longer the sub, the longer the corner-star arcs.

The König relation

The Dec drift rate encodes the alignment error. Ephemeris uses König's method to turn a session's measured drift into an estimate, in arc-minutes:

PA error (arcmin) ≈ 3.82 × |Dec drift ″/min| / cos(δ)

where δ is the target's declination from the log header. The estimate is suppressed near the celestial pole (the cosδ term blows up) and it can't tell you the direction of misalignment — only its size. Details of the computation are in Statistics & drift.

PHD2's three alignment tools

All three live in PHD2's Tools menu:

Drift AlignThe gold standard. Measures drift on a star near the meridian/equator (for the azimuth axis), then near the eastern or western horizon (for altitude), and shows a bullseye to adjust against. Most accurate; takes the longest; needs those two sky regions visible.
Polar Drift AlignPoint at a star near the pole and let PHD2 watch it drift; it computes where the star should sit and you move the mount until it gets there. Simple, one position, no plate solving — good when only the pole region is clear. Slower to converge to high precision.
Static Polar AlignUses known bright stars near the pole and a rotation around the RA axis to find the mount's axis directly — quick, no waiting for drift. Best for getting close fast (or when the pole star is visible but drift time isn't available); finish with a drift method if you need the last arc-minute.

How good is good enough?

  • < 5′ — good. Dec drift stays small, guiding absorbs it without drama, and field rotation is negligible for ordinary sub lengths.
  • 5–10′ — workable but visible: expect persistent one-direction Dec corrections and some rotation in the far corners on long subs.
  • > 10′ — fix it. Guiding spends its effort fighting drift, settling after dithers slows down, and corner stars arc on long exposures no matter how good the RMS looks.
Caveat: an estimate from one session's drift is approximate — refraction and flexure also produce Dec drift. Treat a single night's number as a hint; a repeated number across nights as a finding; PHD2's Drift Align as the measurement.

Where this shows up in Ephemeris

The polar alignment estimate appears with the session statistics, computed from Dec drift via the König relation above. The recommender raises "Dec corrections heavily one-directional" when a session's Dec pulses are dominated by one direction — the steady-drift signature — and points back to this topic. Persistent Dec drift is also one of the causes the gap-analysis observations distinguish in Common patterns.
