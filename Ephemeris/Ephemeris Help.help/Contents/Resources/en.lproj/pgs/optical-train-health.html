Optical-train health

Guiding quality starts before the first correction: a well-focused guide star, dry optics, and a rigid path from guide sensor to imaging sensor.

Focus on the guide camera

PHD2 finds the star by its centroid, and centroid precision depends on the star's profile. A badly focused guide star — large HFD, low peak brightness — gives a mushy centroid that adds measurement noise to every frame, which then gets guided on. Focus the guide camera until the star's HFD is small and stable; near focus is what matters, perfection isn't. Avoid the opposite extreme too: a saturated star clips its profile and degrades the centroid just as surely. PHD2's star profile tool (see the PHD2 manual) shows both problems at a glance.

Dew

Dew forming on the guide optics shows up in the log as a slow, hours-long decline in star mass and SNR — the star dims and bloats until PHD2 starts losing it. Dew on the imaging optics, by contrast, never appears in the guide log at all (see What the guide log can't tell you). Dew heaters on both ends of the train are cheaper than a lost night.

OAG vs guide scope

Off-axis guiderGuides through the imaging optics. Differential flexure is eliminated by construction, and mirror movement is seen and corrected. Costs: smaller star selection at the field edge, fainter stars at long focal ratios, fussier focus, and back-focus to budget.
Guide scopeEasy bright stars, simple setup, no back-focus impact. Costs: the entire differential-flexure failure mode, and blindness to anything that moves only the imaging path. Fine at short imaging focal lengths; increasingly risky past ~1000 mm.

The rule of thumb: the longer the imaging focal length and the heavier the moving mirror, the stronger the case for an OAG.

Mirror flop on SCTs

SCTs and similar designs focus by moving the primary mirror, and a moving mirror can shift on its baffle as the gravity vector changes — crossing the meridian is the classic trigger. The image jumps or drifts while a separate guide scope sees nothing. Telltales: guiding (or image quality) that is consistently different on one pier side, position jumps right after a flip, and trailed subs that an OAG would have caught. Mitigations: use the mirror locks if the OTA has them (focus → lock → trim with an external focuser), guide with an OAG so the flop is at least corrected, and recalibrate per pier side when PHD2 asks for it.

Reading the diagnostics

  • Slow star-mass decline all session — dew or focus drift on the guide optics.
  • Star-mass flicker with steady mean — transparency / seeing, not the train (Seeing vs equipment).
  • Step changes in star mass or position — something mechanical moved: mirror, cable, clamp.
  • Frequent star-lost frames at normal star mass — the star may be too faint or doubled; pick a better one or enable multi-star guiding in PHD2.

Where this shows up in Ephemeris

The Star mass and SNR sub-charts in the document window (Overlays › Diagnostics) are the primary instruments for everything on this page. The recommender's "Pier-side asymmetry detected" observation compares west- and east-side sessions within a night — on an SCT, a persistent asymmetry is one of the few log-visible hints of mirror flop (stale per-side calibration is the other usual suspect). "Star lost on N% of frames" flags acquisition problems, and the discrepancy card "Trailed subs despite sub-pixel guide RMS" covers the failures the guide log can't see directly.
